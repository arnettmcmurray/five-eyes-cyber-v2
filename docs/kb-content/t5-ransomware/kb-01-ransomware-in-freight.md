# Ransomware in Freight: What You're Actually Facing

When ransomware hits a freight carrier, the first thing that disappears isn't files — it's operational capability. Dispatch can't see loads. Drivers can't log hours. Billing can't generate invoices. The TMS is gone. The question stops being "what did they steal?" and becomes "how do we move freight today?"

That's the threat. Not an abstract data breach. A complete operational shutdown at a moment determined by criminals, not you.

## How Bad Is It?

ENISA's 2025 transport sector threat assessment found ransomware accounts for 83.9% of cybercrime against EU transport organizations. IBM X-Force's 2025 threat intelligence report ranks transportation as the fifth most attacked industry globally. Freight isn't a secondary target — it's a primary one.

## What Happened to ORBCOMM

In September 2023, a ransomware attack against ORBCOMM — a major ELD and fleet telematics provider — knocked ELD systems offline across multiple major carriers for approximately three weeks. Drivers lost the ability to record Hours of Service electronically. Fleet managers lost real-time visibility into vehicle locations and status. Dispatch operations reverted to phone and paper. FMCSA issued temporary regulatory exemptions to give carriers time to recover.

Three weeks. For a major technology provider with resources most carriers don't have.

## Why Freight Is a High-Value Target

Ransomware operators pick targets that will pay quickly. Freight meets every criterion:

**Time pressure is built in.** A load with a 48-hour delivery window can't wait for IT to restore systems. Every hour of downtime translates directly into missed pickups, penalties, and shipper relationships at risk. Attackers know this.

**Margins are thin.** Most carriers operate on 3–6% net margins. A week of disruption can erase months of profit. The pressure to pay and resume is enormous.

**IT is decentralized.** A large carrier might have a small IT team covering hundreds of drivers, dozens of terminals, and a patchwork of ELD vendors, TMS platforms, and load board integrations. Independent owner-operators often have no IT at all.

**The network is mixed.** Corporate systems sit alongside carrier-provided apps, driver-owned phones, and vendor telematics systems. Each connection is a potential entry point.

## How Attackers Get In

The attack chain in freight follows a predictable pattern: initial access, lateral movement, then encryption. Initial access usually comes through one of three routes — a phishing email that delivers a malicious payload, an exposed Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) port on a system connected to the internet, or stolen VPN credentials bought on criminal markets. Once inside, attackers move quietly through the network for hours or days, mapping systems, escalating privileges, and positioning themselves before triggering encryption.

The goal is to encrypt everything that matters simultaneously, leaving no clean system to recover from without paying.

Understanding this pattern matters because the window between initial access and encryption is where the attack can still be stopped.
