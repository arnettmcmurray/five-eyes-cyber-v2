# Smishing and Vishing: Mobile Phone Scams for Drivers and Field Staff

Phishing is not just email. Text message scams (smishing) and phone call scams (vishing) target drivers, dock workers, and field operations staff specifically because they are mobile, working under time pressure, and less likely to have IT support nearby. This article covers what these attacks look like in freight and logistics operations and what to do when you encounter them.

## Smishing: Scam Texts Targeting Freight Workers

Smishing is phishing via SMS or messaging apps. Attackers send text messages designed to look like legitimate freight communications.

### Common smishing lures for freight workers

**Fake delivery notifications** — "Your package could not be delivered. Update your delivery address: [link]." These are generic but effective. Dock staff handling multiple carrier apps may click without thinking.

**Fake load board or TMS alerts** — "Alert: Your DAT account has been locked. Verify now: [link]." Dispatchers and owner-operators who manage their own bookings are the target.

**Fake compliance or driver alert** — "ELD malfunction detected on your unit. Confirm your driver ID: [link]." Designed to look like a telematics or ELD vendor notification.

**Fake pay or reimbursement notifications** — "Your direct deposit for [amount] failed. Re-enter your bank details: [link]." Timed near payroll.

**Fake shipper or broker texts** — A text appearing to be from a new broker or shipper with a load offer and a link to "register and accept the load."

### Red flags in text messages

- A link is included and action is urgently required.
- The sender is an unknown number or a number that appears as an email address.
- The message creates fear: account locked, compliance violation, pay failed.
- The message asks for personal information, login credentials, or bank details via text.

**The rule for texts:** Legitimate companies — load boards, TMS vendors, your employer — will not ask you to log in or provide sensitive information through a link in a text message. If a link is included, go to the app or website directly instead.

## Vishing: Phone Call Scams

Vishing (voice phishing) uses phone calls to manipulate victims into revealing information, confirming credentials, or taking actions they otherwise would not. Freight workers are targeted with several specific call types.

### Common vishing scenarios in freight

**"IT support" calls** — A caller claiming to be from your TMS vendor, ELD provider, or company IT says there is a problem with your account and they need your login to fix it. Legitimate IT does not need your password to help you.

**"Dispatcher" impersonation** — A caller pretending to be your dispatch office asks for your current location, load details, or to re-confirm pickup instructions. This is used to gain situational awareness for cargo diversion.

**"Shipper security" calls** — A caller claiming to be from a shipper's loss prevention or security team asks you to verify the load details and your identity — then asks you to wait at a specified location or hold delivery pending verification. This can be a setup for interception.

**"Bank fraud prevention" calls** — A caller claiming to be from your bank or factoring company says there's been suspicious activity and they need to verify your account by having you provide your account number, card, or a one-time code sent to your phone.

**Caller ID spoofing** — The caller's number may show as a real company's number. Caller ID cannot be trusted. A call appearing to come from your broker, your bank, or even your company's own number may not be.

### What to do on a suspicious call

1. **Do not confirm or provide any information** — including your location, load details, passwords, or one-time codes.
2. **Tell the caller you will call them back** on a number you look up yourself from the company's official website or your existing contact records.
3. **Hang up.** Persistent callers who pressure you not to hang up or call back are almost certainly fraudulent.
4. **Report the call** to your supervisor or security contact. Other team members may receive the same call.

## The "Good Samaritan" Scenario

A specific social engineering pattern targets drivers directly. A stranger at a truck stop, fuel station, or dock approaches and offers help — with your route, with a technical problem, or with directions — and casually asks for access to your cab, your device, or your load details. This person may be gathering intelligence for a cargo theft attempt or may be attempting to install something on your device.

Be professionally skeptical of unsolicited in-person help from strangers around freight.

## For Drivers: The Short Version

- A text with a link asking you to log in or give info = suspicious. Go to the app directly.
- A caller asking for your location, your load details, or a code from your phone = suspicious. Hang up and call back through a known number.
- Someone you do not know at the dock, fuel stop, or weigh station who wants to "help" with your load = report to dispatch.
- When in doubt: stop, verify out-of-band (call dispatch on the number in your contacts), then act.
