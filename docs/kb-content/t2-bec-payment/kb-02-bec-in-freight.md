# BEC in Freight: Factoring Fraud, Invoice Redirection, and Executive Impersonation

Business Email Compromise (BEC) is a targeted attack where criminals use email — either by hacking a legitimate account or by spoofing it convincingly — to deceive a company into making unauthorized payments or changing financial account information. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center consistently ranks BEC among the costliest cybercrime categories it tracks, with annual losses in the billions of dollars across all industries.

In freight and logistics, BEC has freight-specific shapes that differ from the generic executive-impersonation scenario. Understanding the patterns that target your actual workflows is how you stop them before they cost you money.

## Pattern 1: Factoring Account Redirect

Freight carriers often use factoring companies to receive payment quickly on invoices. BEC attackers understand this relationship. The attack:

- An attacker compromises the email account of your factoring company, a carrier you work with, or even your own accounts payable staff.
- A convincing email is sent to your AP team announcing "new banking instructions" for a carrier's factoring account.
- The email may include a PDF attachment that looks like an official factoring document.
- Future payments to that carrier go to the attacker's account rather than the carrier.

By the time the legitimate carrier reports non-payment (often weeks later), the money is gone.

## Pattern 2: Vendor or Supplier Invoice Redirect

The Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) has documented this pattern extensively: attackers impersonate a supplier or vendor you pay regularly and send a notice of "new banking details effective immediately" for future invoices.

In freight, targets include:
- Fuel suppliers
- Equipment vendors (trailers, tractors, parts)
- Brokerage partners
- Service providers (insurance, software, maintenance)

The attacker either compromises the vendor's email or creates a convincing spoofed domain. The invoice looks real, the amount looks right, only the bank details are wrong.

## Pattern 3: Executive Impersonation — Urgent Wire Requests

A message appears to be from a company owner, CEO, or senior manager to a finance or administrative employee. It requests an urgent wire transfer, often with language like:
- "I'm in a meeting and can't talk — please wire $XX,XXX to this account immediately."
- "This is confidential — don't discuss with anyone until it's done."
- "I'll explain later — this is time-sensitive."

The request is designed to exploit the employee's desire to be helpful and avoid disappointing a senior leader. The urgency and secrecy are deliberate: they prevent the employee from verifying through normal channels.

## Pattern 4: Detention, Layover, and Fuel Advance Fraud

A fraudulent "carrier" (or a compromised carrier account) requests payment for detention, layover, or a fuel advance before a load is complete. The amount is small enough to avoid triggering approval thresholds but the bank details have been changed from the legitimate carrier's account.

## Pattern 5: Driver Direct Deposit Change

An attacker sends an email to payroll or HR pretending to be a driver requesting a change to their direct deposit bank account. The "driver" provides a new account number to receive their next paycheck.

## What Makes BEC Different From Phishing

Phishing casts a wide net — the same email goes to thousands of people. BEC is targeted. The attacker has researched your company: who handles payments, who your vendors are, what your factoring company is called, the names of your executives. The email is specific to you.

This specificity makes BEC emails much harder to recognize. The sender name looks right. The request is contextually appropriate. Only the small details — the actual email domain, the bank account, the subtle urgency — reveal the fraud.

## The Common Thread Across All BEC Patterns

Every BEC attack has one goal: get money to move before verification happens. The playbook is always:
1. Create plausible context (real company name, real relationship, real process).
2. Add urgency (deadline, hot load, executive pressure, compliance risk).
3. Make verification seem unnecessary or disrespectful (confidentiality request, pressure not to ask).

The defense is the same in every case: **verify independently, using a contact you already have, before any money moves.**
