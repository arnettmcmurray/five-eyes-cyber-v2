# Reporting to Regulators: TSA, CIRCIA, SEC, and NYDFS

Freight operations sit across multiple regulatory jurisdictions. Whether you are a Class I railroad, a publicly traded logistics provider, a freight payment processor, or a mid-size trucking company watching a proposed rulemaking, external reporting obligations are not optional — they are legal requirements with deadlines that start running the moment an incident is discovered or determined to be reportable. Here is what each layer requires, who it applies to, and what the timelines look like.

## TSA Security Directive 1580-21-01 Version C (Current — Freight Railroad and Pipeline)

**Who it covers:** Designated freight railroad operators and pipeline owners/operators identified by TSA. Trucking is not currently covered by this directive — see the November 2024 NPRM section below.

**Effective date:** October 2024, annual renewal. Covered entities should verify the current version directly with TSA each October.

**What it requires:**

- **Cybersecurity coordinator:** Designate a primary cybersecurity coordinator with current contact information available to TSA 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. This person must be reachable during an incident.
- **24-hour incident reporting:** Report significant cybersecurity incidents to CISA within 24 hours of identifying that an incident occurred. This is a hard deadline, not a target.
- **Incident response plan:** Develop and maintain a cybersecurity incident response plan that documents roles, procedures, and communication chains.
- **Annual architecture review:** Conduct an annual review of the organization's cybersecurity architecture.

**What qualifies as a "significant" incident under this directive:** Any incident that disrupts operations, indicates evidence of ongoing unauthorized access, or involves ransomware or a destructive cyberattack. When in doubt, report — the directive does not penalize over-reporting.

## CIRCIA — Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (Proposed — Final Rule Pending)

CIRCIA is law, but the implementing rules are not yet final. As of early 2026, rulemaking is ongoing. Do not state CIRCIA requirements as current obligations — they are not yet enforceable.

**What is proposed:** When finalized, CIRCIA is expected to require covered entities in critical infrastructure sectors — including transportation — to report significant cyber incidents to CISA within 72 hours of determining the incident is reportable. Ransom payments would need to be reported within 24 hours.

**Why this matters before finalization:** Build a CIRCIA reporting step into your incident response plan now. When the rule is finalized, you will need the reporting workflow, the point of contact, and the documentation process already in place. Retrofitting an IR plan under pressure after a breach is not the time to learn what CIRCIA requires.

## SEC Item 1.05 — Form 8-K (Publicly Traded Companies)

**Who it covers:** Any publicly traded logistics provider, carrier, broker, or transportation technology company.

**What it requires:** Material cybersecurity incidents must be disclosed in a Form 8-K filing within 4 business days of the company determining that the incident is material.

**What "material" means:** A reasonable investor would consider the information important when deciding whether to buy, sell, or hold shares. Operational disruption, data loss affecting customers, and financial impact all factor into materiality. A company does not get to delay the 4-day clock by taking an extended period to assess whether something is "really" material.

**Practical implication:** If your company is publicly traded and your TMS is encrypted by ransomware on a Monday, and your leadership determines on Thursday that the incident is material, the 8-K must be filed by the following Wednesday at the latest. Legal and IR teams need to be coordinating simultaneously — disclosure analysis cannot wait until the incident is fully contained.

## NYDFS 23 NYCRR 500 (New York-Licensed Financial Services)

**Who it covers:** Factoring companies, freight payment processors, and other financial services entities licensed by the New York Department of Financial Services.

**What it requires:**
- Notify NYDFS of cybersecurity incidents within 72 hours of determining a reportable event has occurred
- Provide annual cybersecurity training to all employees, including specific coverage of social engineering tactics
- Maintain a written cybersecurity program meeting NYDFS specifications

**Freight relevance:** Freight factoring is a significant segment of the transportation finance market. If your company purchases receivables from carriers or processes freight payments and holds a New York financial services license, NYDFS applies to you regardless of where your trucks run.

## The Practical Sequence for Most Freight Operators

1. **Contain first.** Reporting obligations have deadlines, but filing an inaccurate report because you are still in the middle of the incident creates its own problems. Stabilize the situation, then report with what you know.
2. **Engage legal immediately.** Legal counsel determines which reporting frameworks apply, whether materiality thresholds are met, and what the clock says.
3. **Do not speculate publicly about scope.** Saying "we think only 200 records were affected" before you have completed forensic analysis creates liability when the real number is 20,000. Say what you know. Stop there.

Regulatory reporting is not optional and the clocks run whether or not your incident response is complete. Build the reporting contacts and workflows into your plan before you need them.
