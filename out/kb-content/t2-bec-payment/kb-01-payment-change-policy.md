# Payment Change Verification Policy: The Non-Negotiable Rules

Business Email Compromise (BEC) is the most financially damaging cybercrime category tracked by the FBI. In freight operations, it appears as fraudulent requests to change banking details, redirect factoring payments, or approve urgent wire transfers. This policy defines the non-negotiable controls that must be followed for any payment instruction change.

## Scope

This policy applies to all staff involved in:
- Accounts payable / accounts receivable
- Carrier and vendor payment processing
- Factoring company communications
- Employee direct deposit management
- Any transaction involving a bank detail change or a new payee

## The Non-Negotiable Rules

### Rule 1: No bank detail change by email alone

No change to a bank account number, routing number, wire address, or payment destination is authorized based on an email request alone — regardless of who the email appears to be from.

This applies to:
- Carrier settlement account changes
- Vendor or supplier bank updates
- Factoring company payment routing changes
- Employee direct deposit changes
- Any "new payment instructions" regardless of the stated reason

### Rule 2: Independent callback verification required

Every payment change request requires a callback to a verified contact at the requesting company or person using a phone number from your existing records or the company's official website — **not** a phone number provided in the requesting email.

Do not use "reply to this email" or "call the number in this message" to verify. Those channels are controlled by the attacker if the email is fraudulent.

Steps for callback verification:
1. Locate the organization's verified contact information (prior correspondence, official website, existing vendor file).
2. Call that contact directly.
3. Verbally confirm: the request is genuine, the new payment details are correct, and the reason for the change.
4. Document the call: who you spoke to, when, and their response.

### Rule 3: Dual approval for high-value or unusual changes

Any payment change that meets one or more of the following criteria requires a second approver:
- Dollar value exceeds [establish your company's threshold]
- The request is described as urgent or time-sensitive
- The change involves a new payee with no prior payment history
- The request was received outside of normal business hours or through an unusual channel

Dual approval means two separate employees independently verify and sign off — not one person approving and then forwarding to another for rubber-stamp confirmation.

### Rule 4: Written documentation

All approved payment changes must be documented with:
- The original request (email, message, or call record)
- The callback verification record (who, when, what was confirmed)
- The approver names
- The date and time of execution

This documentation must be retained for a minimum of [establish your retention period].

## What to Do When You Receive a Payment Change Request

1. Do not act on it immediately, regardless of urgency claims.
2. Locate the verified contact information independently.
3. Call and verbally confirm.
4. If the amount or circumstances require dual approval, get the second approval before proceeding.
5. Document everything.
6. If you cannot verify, escalate to your supervisor before proceeding.

## What to Do If You Are Pressured to Bypass These Rules

If someone — including a person claiming to be a senior leader or executive — pressures you to skip verification steps due to urgency, do not comply. Escalate to a second senior person through a different communication channel.

Legitimate payment changes can always withstand verification. Only fraudulent requests require you to skip it.

## If a Fraudulent Payment Was Already Made

See the "If you already sent money" response card immediately. Speed of response is critical — banks can sometimes recover funds from unauthorized transfers within hours, but recovery becomes increasingly difficult after 24–48 hours.
