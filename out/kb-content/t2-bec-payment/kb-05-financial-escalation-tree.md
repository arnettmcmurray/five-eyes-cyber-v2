# Financial Escalation Tree: Who to Call When Payment Fraud Occurs

When a payment fraud attempt is discovered — or when a fraudulent payment has already been made — the sequence and speed of escalation directly affects how much is recoverable. This document defines who to contact, in what order, and what to tell them.

## Step 1: Internal Escalation (Immediate — within minutes)

**Who:** Your direct supervisor, and the company owner or finance leader if not the same person.

**What to say:**
- The approximate amount of the payment at risk or already sent.
- The bank account and routing number the payment was directed to.
- When the payment was initiated or when you discovered the fraud.
- Any communication you have documenting the fraudulent request.

**Goal:** Get a decision-maker involved immediately. The next steps require authorization and access that individual contributors may not have.

## Step 2: Your Bank (Within 1 hour of discovery)

**Who:** Your bank's fraud department or wire recall desk — not the general customer service line.

**What to ask for:** A SWIFT recall or ACH reversal request. Use exact terms:
- For wire transfers: "I need to initiate a wire recall — the transfer was fraudulent."
- For ACH transactions: "I need to request a reversal on a fraudulent ACH debit."

**What to provide:** The wire amount, recipient bank name, recipient account and routing number, transfer date, and reference number.

**Critical timing:** Wire recall success rates drop sharply after 24–48 hours. After 72 hours, international wire recovery is rare. Call immediately.

## Step 3: Recipient Bank (Concurrent with Step 2, if possible)

If you know the receiving bank (often visible from the account/routing number), your bank's fraud team may contact the receiving bank directly. In some cases, having your bank and the receiving bank both on alert simultaneously speeds the freeze.

This is a bank-to-bank action — your institution handles this, but prompt initiation makes it possible.

## Step 4: Your Cyber Insurance Carrier (Within 4 hours)

If you have a cyber liability or crime insurance policy, notify your carrier's claims line as soon as possible. Many policies have reporting windows (24–48 hours) for covered losses, and some carriers have incident response resources they will deploy.

Have ready: your policy number, the loss amount, a summary of what happened, and any documentation.

## Step 5: Law Enforcement — IC3 and FBI (Within 24 hours)

File a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at IC3.gov. The FBI operates the Financial Fraud Kill Chain — a program that works with financial institutions to halt and recover funds in BEC cases. Early filing gives the program more recovery options.

Additionally, file a local police report — this creates a documented record that may be required by your insurance carrier and is useful if the case proceeds to prosecution.

## Step 6: Affected Vendors or Carriers (Coordinated — do not delay)

If a vendor's or carrier's account was the one being impersonated — meaning a fraudulent payment was made in their name to the wrong account — notify them. They need to know their identity was used in the fraud, they may have been compromised themselves, and the legitimate payment they are owed may need to be reprocessed.

Coordinate this notification with your finance leader before contacting vendors, to avoid revealing confidential financial details unnecessarily.

## What Not to Do

- **Do not delete the fraudulent email or communication** — it is evidence.
- **Do not contact the fraudster** back through the email thread — this alerts them and may accelerate fund movement.
- **Do not make additional payments** while the investigation is active unless explicitly authorized by your finance leader.
- **Do not discuss the situation on social media** or in any public channel.

## Evidence to Preserve

From the moment fraud is detected, preserve:
- The original fraudulent email (full headers — not just the body).
- Any attachments that were part of the fraud.
- Your payment records: amount, destination account, timestamp.
- Any phone calls or messages that were part of the fraud attempt.
- Records of who approved the payment and when.

This evidence supports bank recovery attempts, law enforcement investigation, and insurance claims.
