# Incident Communications: What to Say and What Not to Say

During an active cyber incident, what you say — and to whom — is as operationally important as what your IT team does on the technical side. Premature disclosures, speculative statements, and unauthorized communications can increase legal exposure, complicate regulatory reporting, and permanently damage customer relationships. Here is how to handle the questions that will come at you from every direction.

---

**Q: A shipper is calling asking why their load is delayed. Can I mention the cyber incident?**

Not without authorization from leadership and legal. You do not know yet what the company has decided to disclose, to whom, and in what language.

Say: *"We are experiencing a system issue and are working to resolve it. I will have an update on your load status within [X hours]."*

Do not say: "ransomware," "cyberattack," "hack," "breach," or any term that characterizes the nature of the incident. That determination belongs to leadership and legal, not to dispatch or customer service.

---

**Q: A reporter is calling asking about the incident. What do I do?**

Route the call to leadership or your designated communications contact immediately. Do not speculate, confirm, deny, or characterize.

Say: *"We are aware of a situation and our team is actively working on it. We will issue a statement when we have more information."*

Then hang up and immediately notify your supervisor that a reporter called. Do not give your name, do not give your title, and do not confirm or deny any specific detail — including whether an incident occurred at all.

---

**Q: A partner carrier or broker is asking if they are affected by whatever is happening at our company.**

Do not answer without checking with IT/security first. If your systems are shared with or connected to theirs — a shared TMS, EDI connection, or integrated load board API — there may be real exposure. If you answer "no" before IT has assessed that question and you are wrong, you have misled a business partner during a time-sensitive incident.

Say: *"We are still assessing the situation. I will have someone reach out to you directly once we have more information."*

---

**Q: Can I post about the incident on social media?**

No. Not during an active incident. Not on personal accounts. Not as a vague hint. Not as a "thoughts and prayers for my team today" post that prompts questions you cannot answer.

Social media posts during incidents create screenshots that live forever, can be shared with reporters, and may constitute unauthorized disclosure of material nonpublic information if your company is publicly traded. The answer is always no until leadership says otherwise.

---

**Q: Should our employees know what is happening?**

Yes — employees deserve to know when an incident is affecting their work environment or their security. But that communication should come from leadership, through official channels, with approved language. Individual employees speculating in group chats, Slack channels, or parking lot conversations create inconsistent messaging that makes the incident worse.

If employees ask you what is going on: *"Leadership is aware and working on it. We'll have an update soon."* That is the right answer.

---

**Q: When can we talk openly about what happened?**

After all of the following are true:

1. The incident is contained — the attacker no longer has access and systems are being recovered
2. The scope is understood — you know what was affected and what was not
3. Regulatory notifications have been made — CISA, FBI/IC3, SEC, or NYDFS filings are submitted where required
4. Legal has reviewed and cleared the external messaging

Post-incident, transparency with customers and business partners often serves the company better than silence. Customers who learn about a breach from a news article, rather than from you directly, are less forgiving than customers who receive a clear, factual communication with next steps. Timing is everything — but the timing is not your decision to make alone.
