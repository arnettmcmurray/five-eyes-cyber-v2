# Driver PII Was Exposed — Steps to Take Within 48 Hours

You have discovered that driver SSNs, CDL numbers, dates of birth, drug test results, or medical records were exposed — through a misconfigured shared folder, a misdirected email, a compromised system, or unauthorized access. Notification obligations begin running at discovery. Follow these steps in order.

---

**1. Identify exactly what was exposed and how many drivers are affected.**

Write down the answers to these questions right now, on paper:
- Which data categories were exposed? (SSN, CDL, DOB, drug test results, medical records — list each one)
- How many drivers are affected?
- How long was the data accessible or exposed?
- Who could have accessed it? (Everyone with the link? A specific unauthorized user? An unknown attacker?)
- When was it first exposed, and when did you discover it?

You need these answers to assess your notification obligations. Do not wait until you have perfect information — document what you know and flag what you don't know yet.

**2. Contain: remove the data from the exposed location and revoke unauthorized access.**

- Change the sharing setting on any exposed cloud folder to "Specific people" with a named, authorized list — do not delete the folder or its contents
- Revoke any unauthorized user accounts or access credentials that were involved
- If the exposure was via email: do not ask the recipient to delete it — you cannot verify they will, and the obligation exists regardless
- Do not delete logs, access records, or the files themselves — preserve everything as evidence

**3. Notify your cyber insurer and legal counsel — within hours, not days.**

Most cyber insurance policies require prompt notification of potential breach events as a condition of coverage. Call your insurer's claims line now. They may provide breach response legal counsel, which you need for the next step.

Legal counsel will determine which state notification laws apply based on where the affected drivers reside. Most states require notification within 30 to 60 days of discovery; some states (New York, California) require notification as expediently as possible. A few sector-specific frameworks require 72-hour notification. The clock started when you discovered the exposure.

**4. Notify affected drivers — describe what was exposed and what you are doing.**

Each affected driver must receive written notification that includes:
- What information was involved (list specifically: SSN, CDL, DOB, drug test results — do not use vague language)
- When the exposure occurred (dates, or best estimate)
- How the exposure happened (briefly — "a file storage configuration error" is sufficient)
- What you are doing to address it
- What they can do to protect themselves:
  - Place a free fraud alert with all three credit bureaus: Equifax (equifax.com), Experian (experian.com), TransUnion (transunion.com)
  - Consider a credit freeze if SSN was exposed
  - Monitor for unusual activity on credit reports and employment records
  - File an identity theft report at FTC IdentityTheft.gov if fraud occurs

For SSN exposure: offer credit monitoring enrollment as a practical response measure.

**5. File with the FTC, state AG, or other regulators if required.**

Many state breach notification laws require simultaneous notification to the state Attorney General when a threshold number of residents are affected (thresholds vary by state — check with legal counsel). If financial data was also involved, additional regulatory notifications may apply. The FTC does not require breach reports in most cases, but affected individuals can be directed to FTC IdentityTheft.gov for recovery resources.

If you have any reason to believe the exposure was intentional or the data has already been misused, file a report with FBI IC3 at ic3.gov.

**6. Document everything — the full timeline, data involved, and all notifications sent.**

Maintain a written record of:
- The date and time you discovered the exposure
- The date and time each containment action was taken
- The scope of data involved and the number of affected drivers
- The dates notifications were sent to each affected driver
- The content of each notification
- All regulatory or law enforcement filings

This documentation is evidence of reasonable response and is required by your cyber insurer as part of the claims process. It is also your defense if a regulatory investigation or civil claim follows.
