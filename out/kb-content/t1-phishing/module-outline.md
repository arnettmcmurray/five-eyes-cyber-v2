# Module: Phishing and Social Engineering

## Module Purpose
Teach every role in a freight/logistics company to recognize phishing attacks across email, text, and phone — and take the right immediate action. This module covers the "psychology of urgency" that makes freight workers specifically vulnerable.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, learners will be able to:
1. Identify the most common freight-specific phishing lures.
2. Apply a 3-second check before clicking any link.
3. Recognize smishing and vishing patterns relevant to drivers and field staff.
4. Report suspicious communications correctly.
5. Respond correctly if they have already clicked or entered credentials.

## Module Structure

### Lesson 1: What Phishing Looks Like in Freight (5 min)
**KB source:** kb-01-phishing-in-freight.md, kb-06-phishing-ransomware-killchain.md

**Core idea:** Phishing in freight is not generic — it is designed to look like the emails, texts, and calls you already receive at work. A fake load board alert is more dangerous than a fake lottery win because it matches your actual job. Clicking the wrong link does not just compromise your account — it can compromise the whole dispatch system and every load attached to it.

**Scenario:** "You are a dispatcher. At 4:45 PM on a Friday, you receive an email from 'DAT Freight Support' saying your load board account has been locked due to suspicious activity. The link says: 'Verify your account now to unlock active loads.' What do you do?"

Discussion: Why does this work? (Time pressure. Active load risk. Friday urgency. Matches real DAT communications.)

### Lesson 2: Reading an Email Before You Click (5 min)
**KB source:** kb-02-email-red-flags.md, kb-04-safe-link-handling.md

**Core idea:** Three checks take 30 seconds and catch most phishing attempts:
1. Check the actual sender email address — not just the display name.
2. Hover over links to see the real destination before clicking.
3. Ask: did I expect this email? Does the urgency make sense?

**Scenario:** Display a spoofed email with correct company name but wrong domain. Have the learner identify the red flags.

**Practice:** "The email is from 'FMCSA Compliance Team <compliance@fmcsa-dot.net>'. Is this a legitimate FMCSA email?" (No. Official FMCSA is `.dot.gov`.)

### Lesson 3: Texts and Calls — Mobile Threats for Field Staff (5 min)
**KB source:** kb-03-smishing-vishing-drivers.md

**Core idea:** Drivers and dock workers are targeted by text message scams and phone call social engineering. The attack is designed for people working under time pressure away from a desk. Caller ID cannot be trusted. A legitimate company never asks you for a one-time code over the phone.

**Scenario (driver-focused):** "You are at a truck stop. You receive a text from an unknown number: 'DAT alert: Your carrier account has been locked. Tap here to restore access before losing active loads.' What do you do?"

**Scenario (call-focused):** "You receive a call from someone claiming to be from your ELD provider. They say your device is showing a malfunction and they need your driver ID and login to push a fix remotely. What do you do?"

### Lesson 4: How to Report — and Why It Matters (3 min)
**KB source:** kb-05-how-to-report-suspicious.md

**Core idea:** Reporting is the most important security action. One report can stop an active attack targeting your whole team. There is no penalty for a false alarm. The cost of not reporting a real attack is far higher than the cost of reporting something harmless.

**Key messages:**
- Do not forward the suspicious email to coworkers — this spreads the attack.
- Preserve the evidence — do not delete it.
- If you already clicked or responded — report immediately. This is especially important.

### Lesson 5: Decision Under Pressure — The Urgency Trap (2 min)
**Core idea:** Every freight attack uses time pressure. "The load will be cancelled." "Your authority is suspended." "The wire must be sent today." The urgency itself is a weapon. When you feel urgency, that is the signal to slow down and verify — not to act faster.

The 60-second pause — to check the sender, hover the link, or call the vendor on a known number — is the entire defense.
